# Chapter 5 summary - Partial observation, Bayesian learning, risk, and regime switching

The physical forestry-market state is not known exactly, and neither are the
parameters that govern growth, bucking, processor acceptance, operating cost,
or observation error. The sufficient decision state is therefore a posterior
law over both latent physical state and structural parameters.

This converts the problem into a Bayes-adaptive control problem. Given the
current posterior and an action, the state and observation kernels generate a
predictive distribution; Bayes' rule maps the next observation into a new
posterior. Under standard measurability assumptions, the posterior is a
controlled Markov state, so history can be compressed into belief.

The finite-horizon Bellman recursion chooses sensing, experiments, market
exposure, contract variables, coverage intensity, and discrete regime actions
by balancing current loss against expected continuation value.

The chapter also states a Wasserstein-space HJB equation for the smoother
conditional biological-law subproblem. This exposes the Lions derivatives and
the second-order term generated by common noise, but the paper does not claim a
closed-form HJB for the entire partially observed market system.

Risk is added through nested expectation-CVaR recursion and distributional
robustness through Wasserstein ambiguity sets. These are different forms of
uncertainty: posterior uncertainty reflects what the current model does not
know; distributional robustness protects against model misspecification around
the posterior predictive law.

Because actions affect future information, the framework contains dual control.
A sensing or contract experiment can be worthwhile even when its immediate
payoff is lower, if it changes future decisions enough. The chapter distinguishes
information gain from decision-focused value such as EVSI.

Finally, large discrete changes in observation or coverage capability are
represented as impulse/regime-switching decisions. The value of waiting is then
an optimal-stopping problem rather than an arbitrary rollout rule.
